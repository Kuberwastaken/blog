
Taste has become more visible because AI has made output abundant.

If anyone can generate ten versions, the important question is no longer who can produce. It is who can choose.

This is where the internet starts to feel different.

You see it first in websites. Not because AI cannot make good websites. It absolutely can. Some of the best AI-assisted websites will be better than what most people could make manually. The mistake is to say AI-coded means bad.

It does not.

But the average AI-coded website often has a recognizable smell.

The same rounded cards. The same glowing gradients. The same “transform your workflow” sentence. The same hero section with too many words and not enough conviction. The same testimonial cards from people who feel like they were generated by a committee of SaaS landing pages. The same icons. The same smooth, competent emptiness.

It is not that any one detail gives it away. It is the accumulation.

The site is polished, but it does not seem to want anything.

That is what is missing: not effort, exactly, but intent.

A website made with effort, even when imperfect, often contains some local decision that does not generalize. A strange spacing choice. A joke in the footer. A color that should not work but somehow does. A button label that sounds like the founder wrote it at 3 a.m. These things are not always optimal, but they are alive. They show that someone was there.

AI can absolutely help create that. But only if there is a person with taste steering it.

Without that, AI does what averaging systems naturally do. It moves toward the plausible. The plausible is useful. The plausible is often the right starting point. But if you stop there, you get something worse than ugly.

You get bland.

This is what I call Ageusia.

Ageusia is the loss of taste. In medicine, it means losing the ability to taste food. On the internet, it is the loss of aesthetic and intentional sensitivity caused by too much frictionless generation.

It is what happens when people confuse output with creation.

AI images made this obvious first.

When they arrived, they felt impossible. Suddenly anyone could generate a cinematic portrait, a fantasy landscape, a product shot, a fake historical scene. It was magical. It still is magical. There is something genuinely beautiful about a person being able to externalize an image in their head without needing years of technical training.

But then people started noticing.

The hands. The teeth. The skin. The soft waxy lighting. The strange posture. The object melting into the background. The amber tint. The face that is almost expressive, but not quite. The image had artifacts, and once people learned to see them, they became hard to unsee.

The same thing happened with AI video.

When Sora 2-style videos first became realistic, the feeling was not aesthetic. It was existential. The gap between fake and real seemed to close overnight. It was scary because the brain’s first reaction was: this is it, we lost.

But then something interesting happened.

People started seeing the seams.

Not just AI researchers. Kids saw them. Older people saw them. Normal people with no technical background saw them. They noticed the rhythm, the shortcuts, the too-clean motion, the almost-perfect audio sync, the way physics looked right until it suddenly didn’t, the way people in the video performed reality without quite inhabiting it.

Even the exact videos that once felt terrifying started to look different later. The video had not changed. The viewer had.

That is important.

Humans are pattern-seeing machines. We have evolved for a very long time by noticing tiny anomalies. A sound in the grass. A false smile. A rotten smell. A sentence that does not match a face. We are not perfect detectors, but we are relentless ones.

The internet trains this instinct collectively.

A few people notice a pattern. They point it out. Others learn it. The pattern becomes a meme, then a heuristic, then almost a reflex. What once required expertise becomes common sense. The collective eye sharpens.

This is why I am more optimistic than the word “slop” usually allows.

There is a cynical view that AI content will flood everything and people will simply accept it because it is cheap. Maybe some of that will happen. But I don’t think humans are passive enough for that to be the whole story.

If something is widespread enough, people develop a taste against it.

The first time you see a cinematic AI drone shot of a futuristic city, it is magic. The thousandth time, it is wallpaper. The first AI-generated product ad feels impressive. The hundredth has the texture of a stock photo pretending to dream. The first vibe-coded landing page feels like superpowers. The thousandth makes you close the tab before reading the headline.

This becomes an economic problem.

Slop can be cheap to produce and still expensive to make people care about. Attention is not infinite. If enough people begin to feel the same micro-disgust toward a certain synthetic texture, the cost of overcoming that disgust rises.

People may not reject AI content because it is AI.

They may reject it because it has no intent.

That distinction matters.

The future is not “human-made good, AI-made bad.” That is too simple, and also obviously false. Some human-made things are terrible. Some AI-assisted things are brilliant. The real divide is between work with taste and work without it.

AI does not remove the need for taste. It makes taste the main thing.

The best builders in the AI age will not be the ones who merely use AI the most. They will be the ones with the strongest preferences. The ones who can look at a generated interface and say: no, this is fake-clean. The ones who delete the impressive paragraph because it says nothing. The ones who ask for twenty versions and then pick the least obvious one. The ones who know when “better” means simpler, stranger, quieter, rougher, or more specific.

AI makes execution abundant. Abundance makes curation sacred.

This is probably why people keep mentioning taste when they talk about AI. They know, even if they do not always say it directly, that the model can generate but cannot care on your behalf. It can produce options, but it cannot know which option feels like you unless you do.

Taste is not just having opinions about fonts or colors. It is a model of what is alive.

It is the ability to detect when something was made by averaging instead of choosing. It is knowing when an image has lighting but no atmosphere, when a video has realism but no presence, when a website has polish but no promise. It is understanding that something can be technically excellent and still dead.

That is the risk of Ageusia.

Not AI. Not automation. Not vibe coding.

The risk is losing the habit of choosing.

The risk is becoming so used to frictionless generation that we accept the first plausible answer as the final one. The risk is forgetting that creation is not the same as completion. The risk is letting the model’s default become our preference.

But Ageusia is not inevitable.

In fact, AI may produce the opposite. It may make taste more valuable than ever.

When everyone can make, the people who know what should be made become rare. When everyone can generate, the people who can recognize what is worth keeping become powerful. When everyone has infinite output, the scarce resource is a refined palette.

The effort does not disappear. It moves.

From typing to choosing.

From implementing to directing.

From making pixels appear to deciding which pixels deserve to stay.

From proving you can build to proving you can care.

The old internet rewarded effort because effort was scarce. The AI internet will reward intent because output is infinite.

And maybe that is the optimistic version of all this.

AI gives more people the ability to create. Then the internet gets flooded with things made without much intent. Then people get tired of them. Then taste becomes visible again. Not as elitism, but as orientation. Not as gatekeeping, but as the thing that prevents everything from collapsing into the same pleasant average.

The most human part of making may turn out not to be labor.

It may be preference.

Preference is not small. Preference is compressed experience. It is memory with an opinion. It is the record of everything you have loved, hated, copied, regretted, and outgrown.

In the AI age, your taste is your checksum.

It proves there is someone there.
